Durchfahrt

An all-electric bullet train that crosses the Philippine archipelago without stopping, and the seventh of the Design Studies. It runs from Luzon down a spine through Panay, Negros, Cebu and Bohol to Mindanao, with branches east to Leyte and Samar and west to Palawan. It adds to the planes and the ferries rather than replacing them, and when a typhoon grounds both, it becomes the country’s lifeline. Durchfahrt is German for passing straight through.

How it runs

The express never stops. Everything that comes and goes does it at speed.

Destination carsWhole cars for the big cities ride at the back. Before each one’s station, its passengers are already aboard; it lets go, drops back and brakes alone onto the side track.
Mixed slip podsShorter cars with all three classes above and a freight hold below. They slip off at the smaller stops, which alternate expresses serve in pairs.
Catch-up podsA pod waits on a side track several kilometres long. As the express nears, it runs up to the same speed beside it, crosses over behind, and latches on to the last car.
BranchesBlue cars for Leyte and Samar and green cars for Palawan ride as blocks behind the gold ones and split off at Maasin and Bongabong without slowing, the rear block first.
Who drivesThe train drives, latches and slips itself. A driver up front supervises from the cab, confirms when the sensors disagree, and can take over with the red handle.

Keeping the line clear

Something on the rails at full speed is the one accident a non-stop line can’t afford. So the line is protected in layers, and it watches itself.

Nothing gets onThe whole line is fenced and either raised or sunk, with no level crossings anywhere. Bridges have walls, typhoon country has wind walls, and the power rail is covered.
The line watches itselfCameras and radar on low, storm-proof posts every 250 m along the fence, each looking 400 m along the track both ways; about 9,600 of them. A fibre-optic cable in the track slab feels footsteps, falling rocks and trees along every metre. There are no camera gantries over the rails: in a typhoon they would be as vulnerable as overhead wires.
Warning in time
If something is hit anywayA steel deflector under the nose and at both ends of every pod pushes debris aside. L-shaped guides on every bogie hook inside the rails and low kerbs in the slab keep a derailed train on its track, as the Shinkansen added after a derailment in the 2004 Niigata earthquake.
After a stormBefore the first express, an unmanned sweep pod runs the line at 120 km/h with a plough, brushes and cameras, and drones fly the bridges, much as the Shinkansen sends an inspection car out every morning.

Facts behind it

What the design leans on, and where it goes past what’s been built.

Slip coachesFrom 1858 to 1960, British and Irish expresses dropped their last coach at speed: a guard in it pulled the coupling free and braked it into the station by hand. The last one was slipped at Bicester in September 1960. Nothing ever latched on at speed; Durchfahrt’s catch-up pods are new.
Third railA shoe on a ground-level rail is proven to about 160 km/h; the end ramps knock the shoe at higher speeds (the record is 175 km/h, in 1988). Durchfahrt’s rail is continuous and enclosed, with no ramps, so it can run faster, but that is untested. Study C shows the line at today’s proven 160 km/h.
TyphoonsMost landfalls are on the Pacific side: Eastern Visayas, Bicol and northern Luzon. The central Visayas are crossed too (Haiyan in 2013, Odette in 2021). Mindanao is mostly south of the belt but not spared (Sendong’s floods in 2011, Pablo in 2012).
WindHigh-speed lines slow down and then stop in strong winds, at roughly 20–30 m/s. So rescue mode runs before landfall and right after it, and holds in tunnels and storm-shelter stations while the eye passes.
Deep waterThe deepest working undersea rail tunnel, Seikan in Japan, reaches about 240 m below the sea. The Semirara and Mindoro crossings are longer and deeper than anything built so far; the Tañon tunnel is shorter, but about as deep.

About

Durchfahrt is an archipelago train by Andre Parrocho, made in November 2026 as the seventh of the Design Studies. It links the places: it runs beneath Denkraum’s Manila tower, past Geborgenheit’s highlands in Bukidnon, and its freight pods can carry Heimweg between the islands. Coastlines are from Natural Earth (public domain).

It’s a design exploration, not an engineering proposal. Distances are measured on the drawn line; times are worked out from the speed you choose.
